# Category Creation Workshop

In a crowded market, you have two options: compete on "better" or win on "different." Competing on better is a race to the bottom — there's always someone cheaper, faster, or louder. Creating a category means you're not competing at all. You're the only option for a specific type of person with a specific type of problem, described in language only you use.

This isn't about inventing something from nothing. It's about reframing existing problems in ways nobody else has — then owning that frame completely.

---

## Why Category Creation Works

### The Commodity Trap
Most solopreneurs position themselves inside existing categories:
- "I'm a business coach"
- "I'm a copywriter"
- "I teach digital marketing"
- "I sell productivity templates"

The problem: inside an established category, you're instantly compared to everyone else who says the same thing. The buyer's decision becomes "who's cheapest?" or "who has the most followers?" You're a commodity.

### The Category Advantage
When you create a new category, you become:
- **The first** (no direct comparison)
- **The authority** (you named the thing)
- **The default** (the category IS you)

Examples of category creation in the solo space:
- "Digital Writing" (Dickie Bush & Nicolas Cole — reframed blogging + social media writing as a distinct discipline)
- "Productized Service" (reframed freelancing into repeatable, packaged offerings)
- "Solopreneurship" (reframed freelancing/self-employment as a systems-driven business)
- "Build in Public" (reframed sharing your work-in-progress as a marketing strategy)

Each of these took an existing activity and gave it a new name, a new framework, and a new identity.

## The Category Creation Process

### Step 1: Map the Existing Landscape

Before you create something new, understand what exists. Map:

**Current category labels:** What do people call what you do?
- List every term your competitors use to describe themselves
- Note which terms feel generic, overused, or commoditized

**Existing positioning angles:** How do competitors differentiate?
- List the common claims: "best," "fastest," "most affordable," "proven"
- Identify what everyone says (this is what you'll avoid)

**Audience frustrations with current options:**
- What do buyers complain about in existing solutions?
- Where do current categories fail to capture what's really happening?
- What assumptions does the existing category carry that aren't true for everyone?

**Ask:** "What does everyone in your space claim — and what's the implicit assumption behind those claims that doesn't hold up?"

### Step 2: Find the Category Gap

The gap is the space between what exists and what your ideal customer actually needs — described in a way nobody else has articulated.

**Three types of category gaps:**

**1. The Underserved Subset**
A group within a broader category that's been ignored or poorly served.

- Broad category: "Business coaching"
- Superniche: "Systems design for solo creators earning $100K-$500K" (not startups, not corporations — a specific in-between)

**2. The Methodology Gap**
A new approach to solving an old problem that hasn't been named.

- Old approach: "Time management tips"
- New category: "Energy management for knowledge workers" (the problem isn't time — it's energy allocation)

**3. The Integration Gap**
Combining two things that haven't been combined before.

- Category A: "Content marketing"
- Category B: "Psychology / behavioral science"
- New category: "Behavioral content strategy" (using psychological triggers in content design)

**Ask:** "What two things do you naturally combine that nobody else connects?"

### Step 3: Create the Language

This is the most important step. A category doesn't exist until it has a name.

**Naming principles:**

- **Two-word power:** The strongest categories are two words that create a new concept. "Digital Writing." "Strategic Intervention." "Growth Hacking."
- **Avoid jargon:** The name should be immediately understandable to a stranger
- **Imply the benefit:** The name should hint at what the category delivers
- **Be specific enough to exclude:** If everyone could claim it, it's not a category
- **Be broad enough to own:** If it only describes one product, it's not a category — it's a product name

**Naming exercise:**

Generate 10-20 candidate names using these formulas:
- [Adjective] + [Existing Category] → "Lean Writing," "Strategic Coaching"
- [New Term] + [Familiar Term] → "Digital Writing," "Revenue Architecture"
- [Outcome] + [Method] → "Wealth Stacking," "Authority Engineering"
- [Philosophy] + [Practice] → "Intentional Marketing," "Mindful Scaling"

Then test each against:
- Can I explain what this means in one sentence?
- Does it feel different from what already exists?
- Would my ideal customer immediately understand it?
- Can I build a body of work around this term?
- Does searching for it return zero or minimal existing results?

### Step 4: Build the Category Framework

A category needs structure — not just a name. Create a proprietary framework that gives the category intellectual weight.

**Framework components:**

**Core thesis:** The one-sentence belief that underpins the category.
- "Digital Writing thesis: The internet rewards writing that is clear, specific, and formatted for screens — not writing that follows academic or journalistic conventions."

**Key principles:** 3-5 rules or beliefs that define how the category operates.
- "Principle 1: Clarity beats cleverness"
- "Principle 2: Format is a feature, not decoration"
- "Principle 3: Atomic ideas compound faster than long-form"

**Proprietary methodology:** A named process or system that only you teach.
- "The 1/3/1 Writing Rhythm" (a structural framework for posts)
- "The Content Compound Effect" (how small posts build into authority)

**Category language glossary:** Key terms you've coined or redefined.
- Catalog every term unique to your category
- Define each one clearly
- Use them consistently across all content

### Step 5: Claim the Category

Owning a category means consistently being the person who defines and evolves it.

**Content strategy for category ownership:**
- Regularly publish "what [category name] is and isn't" content
- Create comparison content: "[Category] vs. [old way]"
- Publish case studies using your category's language
- Develop a signature talk, essay, or manifesto for the category
- Build a glossary or "how to get started with [category]" resource

**Authority signals:**
- Be the person who named it (always reference the origin)
- Train others in the framework (your students become ambassadors)
- Evolve the category publicly (add new concepts, refine principles)
- Reference your own language consistently (repetition creates recognition)

### Step 6: Position Against, Not Within

The final step is positioning your category explicitly against the status quo — not within it.

**Positioning formula:**
"[Old category] focuses on [old approach]. [New category] focuses on [new approach]. The difference is [specific, tangible contrast]."

**Example:**
"Traditional business coaching focuses on growth at all costs. Sovereign Business Design focuses on building a profitable, one-person operation that maximizes freedom over headcount. The difference is you end up with a business that serves your life — not one that consumes it."

## Example

**User:** "I help people write better online content, but so does everyone."

### Category Gap
Everyone teaches "content marketing" or "copywriting." But what most solo creators actually need isn't marketing theory or ad copy — it's a writing practice optimized for screens, algorithms, and human attention spans. That's a different discipline.

### Category Definition
**Name:** Attention Architecture
**One-sentence definition:** The practice of designing written content for maximum cognitive impact in screen-based environments.
**Core thesis:** Attention is the scarcest resource online. Writing that respects attention — through structure, rhythm, and clarity — outperforms writing that fights for it through volume and noise.

### Positioning
"Content marketing focuses on distribution and volume. Attention Architecture focuses on the structural design of each piece — making every sentence fight for its place. The difference: you don't need more content, you need content that's architecturally impossible to scroll past."

## Important Notes

- **Category creation is not rebranding.** It's not just calling the same thing a new name. The category must represent a meaningfully different approach.
- **The language IS the strategy.** Whoever controls the language controls the category. Name things. Define terms. Create a glossary.
- **You don't need permission.** You don't need to be the world's #1 expert. You need to be the first person to name and structure the category.
- **Categories compound slowly.** This is a long game — 6-12 months before the category takes hold. But once it does, you're un-commoditizable.
- **Not every business needs a new category.** If you can dominate an existing subcategory, that works too. Category creation is for when existing categories flatten you into a commodity.
- **Test the name with real humans.** If your ideal customer doesn't immediately get it, iterate.
